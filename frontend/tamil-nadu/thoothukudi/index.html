A Port City Where Salt Is Part of the Process

Why Thoothukudi pipework needs a different grade of thinking

Thoothukudi, also written Tuticorin, sits on the Gulf of Mannar coast. V.O. Chidambaranar Port handles bulk and container cargo. The area is a major salt-producing region, with pans along the coast. Around the port are thermal power, chemical and fertiliser plants, seafood processing and export units, and growing wind and solar activity along the coast. Water supply for many of these industries depends on seawater treatment or desalination.

That adds up to a heavy load on metal. Chloride, in air, brine and cleaning water, is the main enemy of stainless steel. Standard 304 resists many environments but can pit in constant chloride exposure. 316 and 316L contain molybdenum, which improves resistance to pitting, and they are the usual choice near the sea.

Nothing about this is exotic to people who work here. What buyers need from a supplier is straight advice on grades, parts that match the thread, and honest word on delivery to the far south.

Corrosion Checklist

Four Questions Before You Order

Written for coastal, chloride-heavy conditions

01

How much chloride is it exposed to?

Direct seawater, brine, salt spray and wash-down with saline water all demand 316 or 316L. A sheltered indoor line away from salt air may do fine in 304. If unsure, tell us site distance from the shoreline.

02

Is the nipple welded or threaded in?

Threaded joints form narrow root crevices where chlorides concentrate, provoking crevice corrosion. For maximum life near the sea, choose 316L, seal threads fully, and prevent stagnant salt pockets.

03

What's the thread?

Imported marine and desalination packages frequently use American NPT, while domestic utility lines use British BSPT. They are not interchangeable. Send a photo or machine part number.

04

Does it ship in a container?

Tell us if the parts are being packed into a container for export, offshore platforms, or transhipment. We apply seaworthy protective packaging accordingly.

Environmental Hazards

What Salt, Brine and Wash-Down Do to Metal

Five hazards, and how to choose around them

Chloride pitting

Salt air and brine attack the passive chromium oxide film on stainless. 304 will pit and develop rust streaks over time. 316 and 316L resist this due to molybdenum.

Crevice corrosion in threads

Threads form microscopic gaps that trap salt water. Apply thread sealant comprehensively to fill thread roots and avoid open crevices.

Wash-down in seafood plants

Continuous cleaning with chlorinated water and caustic sanitizers stresses joints. Choose smooth turned finishes, fewer fittings, and 316 grade.

Mixed metals in salt air

Stainless in direct contact with carbon steel or galvanised iron in coastal humidity rapidly corrodes the less noble metal via galvanic action. Isolate dissimilar metals.

Salt crust and solar heat

Salt deposits crystallize on piping exposed to sun and wind. Rinse exposed fittings periodically and perform scheduled visual inspections.

Field Practices

Fitting Stainless Nipples in Salt Air

Habits that extend life near the coast

  • Seal every thread fully: Apply PTFE tape or anaerobic paste comprehensively so salt water cannot wick into the thread roots.
  • Use marine-compatible anti-seize: Prevents thread galling on stainless fittings that must be disassembled for inspection.
  • Don't over-tighten: Damaged threads create micro-fissures where chlorides concentrate and accelerate pitting.
  • Eliminate carbon steel cross-contamination: Never use rusted iron wrenches on stainless parts without protective pads.
  • Rinse and dry exposed joints: Flush joints with clean fresh water after outdoor assembly if coastal sea spray is active.
  • Support outdoor spans: Wind buffeting and valve weight on coastal jetties loosen joints unless properly braced.
  • Inspect on a schedule: Look for salt crusting, tea-staining, and weeping at thread shoulders during routine rounds.
